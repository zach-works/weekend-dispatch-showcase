Bird of the Week
Cedar Waxwing
Bombycilla cedrorum — A sleek, crested fruit-eater that travels in flocks from berry tree to berry tree
Where
Anywhere with fruit: crabapples, hollies and junipers in parks and on streets.
ID
Silky tan-grey with a crest, a black mask, a bright yellow tail tip, and small waxy red tips on the wing feathers.
Voice
Very high, thin whistles, often the first clue a flock is overhead.
Range
Across most of North America; in NYC they can turn up in any month.
Conservation angle

A living indicator of what we plant: birds that eat non-native honeysuckle berries while growing tail feathers can have orange tail tips instead of yellow.

Tree of the Week
Sitka Spruce
Picea sitchensis — The giant of the Pacific fog belt, and the largest spruce in the world
Where
A narrow coastal strip from Alaska to northern California, rarely far from salt spray and fog. On Vancouver Island, the Carmanah Valley holds some of the biggest.
Needles
Stiff, flattened and sharp enough to hurt when you grab a branch, with whitish bands underneath.
Cones
Light and papery, with thin, wavy-edged scales.
Bark
Thin and grey-brown, flaking off in small, round scales.
Conservation angle

Its wood is light and strong for its weight, which made it the favoured timber for early aircraft frames and still makes it the classic soundboard for guitars and pianos. Old-growth coastal stands like Carmanah were protected only after long campaigns against logging.

Map of the Week
Prospect Heights breaks the 10-20-30 rule
291 living street trees on a USGS aerial photo

Urban foresters use Santamour’s 10-20-30 rule as a hedge against pests: no more than 10% of a street-tree population from one species, 20% from one genus, 30% from one family. Here, thornless honeylocust (12.7%) breaks the species line. The genus Quercus (23.4%) also passes 20%.

Then & Now
viii.
Wallabout Bay and the prison ships
Then

The bay took its name from the Walloon settlers who farmed its shore in the 1600s. During the Revolution, the British moored prison ships there, and more than 11,000 American captives died aboard them.

Now

The shoreline became the Brooklyn Navy Yard in 1801, which built the USS Missouri, where Japan’s surrender was signed in 1945. It closed as a Navy yard in 1966 and is now an industrial park. The prisoners are remembered by the Prison Ship Martyrs’ Monument in Fort Greene Park.

Portland Head Light
Cape Elizabeth, Maine — one of the oldest lighthouses in the country
Built
First lit in January 1791, early in George Washington’s presidency.
Setting
A white tower on a rocky headland at the entrance to Portland Harbor, now inside Fort Williams Park.
Fame
It is often called the most photographed lighthouse in America.
Today
Still an active aid to navigation, with a museum in the keepers’ house.

Trip 1 · Metro-North · Harlem Line
Botanical Garden
The New York Botanical Garden is a short walk from this Bronx stop, and the ride is under half an hour

The closest trip in the library: 250 acres including the Thain Family Forest, a surviving piece of the forest that covered the region before the city. The Enid A. Haupt Conservatory, a Victorian glasshouse, makes this a good wet-weather outing too.

RideAbout 25 minutes from Grand Central, with trains about every half hour on weekends.

On footThe Garden is about a ten-minute walk from the platform.

This weekendThe overpass at Botanical Garden is closed. Use Bedford Park Blvd instead. (July 21 - mid December)

Glen Cove
A small museum of Long Island geology and archaeology on the shore of Long Island Sound

Garvies Point Museum and Preserve, run by Nassau County, covers Long Island's geology and its Native American archaeology, and the preserve around it has trails along the Sound.

Trip 3 · NJ Transit · Hoboken Terminal
Hoboken Terminal
A Beaux-Arts railroad terminal and the best view of the Manhattan skyline from New Jersey

Hoboken Terminal, opened in 1907 for the Delaware, Lackawanna & Western Railroad, still has its Beaux-Arts waiting room, ferry slips and train sheds. Walk out onto Pier A Park and along the Hudson River Waterfront Walkway to Sinatra Park, with the skyline across the water.

RideAbout half an hour from New York Penn Station, usually with a change at Secaucus: use NJ Transit's trip planner. The PATH train from 33rd Street is the other way in.

On footPier A Park is a 4-minute walk and Sinatra Park about 11 minutes from the terminal. The Hoboken Historical Museum is about 1.5 miles.

This weekendNJ Transit publishes no alerts feed we can read: check njtransit.com for weekend service changes.
